A Fast and Short Algorithm for Induction

Sigfrido D. Ciletti

This page presents an algorithm, devised by the author, for predicting a binary sequence with bounded computation. The programs of a small monotone reference machine are explored as a tree of prefixes and run by dovetailing, so that programs sharing a prefix share their computation. A prefix is rejected, together with every program extending it, as soon as its output contradicts the observed sequence, and the probability of the next symbol is the a priori weight, 2−ℓ, of the surviving programs that have already produced it, renormalized among them. A prediction is available at every moment, and it approaches Solomonoff’s prediction over the programs of the machine as computation increases.

The novelty lies in separating search from belief. A learned guiding distribution may decide where computation is spent, but it never enters the probabilities, and every program not yet rejected is guaranteed at least half of the computation its a priori probability entitles it to. Learning can therefore accelerate the search, but it can slow it by at most a factor of two and cannot alter the weight of any program. To the author’s knowledge this separation has not been made before: in Solomonoff’s incremental learning systems the guiding distribution also serves as the prior, and in Schmidhuber’s speed prior the cost of computation enters the probabilities themselves.

The reference machine

The reference machine has a circular tape of 256 binary cells and the six instructions listed below, each with the length of its code. The machine reads its program one instruction at a time, and only when execution requires the next one. Programs that share an initial segment therefore share the corresponding computation.

.write the content of the current cell to the output2 bits
+complement the current cell2 bits
>move one cell to the right3 bits
<move one cell to the left3 bits
[mark a position for return3 bits
]if the current cell contains 1, return to the most recent mark, or to the beginning if there is none3 bits

Rejection and prediction

A program is rejected as soon as it writes a symbol that contradicts the observed sequence, and with it every program of which it is an initial segment. The probability assigned to the next symbol is the normalized sum of the a priori probabilities, 2−ℓ, of the surviving programs that have already produced that symbol.

Computation

No bound on computation is fixed in advance. The search proceeds continuously, occupying about two milliseconds of each display frame, and a symbol is revealed every 250 milliseconds, so that the computation devoted to each symbol is whatever the device accomplishes in that interval. In the trials conducted so far, greater computation per symbol has never delayed convergence. Trials should be compared in machine steps, a measure that does not depend on the device.

The guiding distribution

When the guiding distribution is in use, the computation is divided equally. One half is allotted in proportion to the a priori probabilities, which bounds the time required by a small constant factor relative to Levin’s universal search. The other half is allotted according to the guiding distribution, a table of conditional instruction probabilities estimated from programs that predicted at least three successive symbols correctly. The guiding distribution determines only the order of search; it has no effect upon the probabilities assigned.

The guiding distribution is not used by default. Within a single trial its effect is negligible; it is of value only across trials, for longer periods, when computation is scarce, as on a slow device. It continues to be estimated while not in use.

Guarantees

Two properties follow from the construction. First, the probabilities depend only on the a priori distribution and on which programs have been examined; the guiding distribution affects only the latter. Second, because every program not yet rejected receives at least half of its a priori share of computation, the time required to reach any program exceeds that of the unguided search by at most a factor of two. As computation increases without bound every program of the machine is eventually examined, and the prediction tends to Solomonoff’s normalized prediction over the programs of at most 32 instructions.

Relation to earlier work

The mixture over programs is Solomonoff’s (1964). The schedule of computation follows Levin’s universal search (1973), and the treatment of programs as prefixes executed online follows Schmidhuber’s FAST algorithm (2002), which he also applied to prediction, though under the speed prior, whose probabilities depend on running time. Solomonoff’s later proposals for incremental learning (2002–2003) introduced a guiding distribution for the search that is revised as problems are solved; there, however, the revised distribution is also the prior. The title alludes to Hutter’s “The Fastest and Shortest Algorithm for All Well-Defined Problems” (2002); no optimality result of that kind is claimed here.

Measures

The log loss is the sum, over the observed symbols, of −log2 of the probability assigned to each, with every probability restricted to the interval from 0.01 to 0.99. The system is said to have converged when eight successive symbols have each been assigned a probability of at least 0.9.

Limitations

  • Observations are assumed to be free of error. A single erroneous symbol would reject the correct program.
  • Programs are limited to 32 instructions.
  • The programs of greatest weight are retained in memory, from 3,000 at the outset, growing by one program for every 1,000 machine steps, to at most 20,000. A program beyond that limit must be found again by the search from the root, whose budget doubles on each pass.
  • A trial is suspended after every 64 symbols.
